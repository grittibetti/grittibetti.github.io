Research/Mutual Information Surprise

Real-time · drifting data · autonomy

From reaction to reflection.

Not every unusual observation matters, and not every important one looks unusual. Mutual Information Surprise asks whether new data actually changed what an autonomous system knows.

Yinsong Wang · Quan Zeng · Xiao Liu · Yu DingINFORMS Journal on Data Science · 2026

Project 02

When should a machine change its mind?

Autonomous systems are increasingly running experiments on their own: a self-driving lab tuning a chemical recipe, or a mobile sensor mapping air pollution across a city. They can move and measure, but their decision logic is usually a fixed rule. A key question is when the system should stop and say, "something has changed, I need a new strategy."

The standard trigger is surprise. But the data these systems collect are real-time, noisy, and drifting, and the classical definitions of surprise can be fooled by exactly that.

What people usually do

Shannon surprise asks, "how unlikely was this observation?" It flags rare events, which means it overreacts to noise and outliers (Case A) and can miss quiet but important changes (Case B). Bayesian surprise measures how much the model's beliefs moved, which is better, but it still does not directly ask whether the system now understands the input–output relationship any better.

Our idea: surprise as a sign of learning

We proposed Mutual Information Surprise (MIS). Instead of "was that unlikely?", MIS asks "did I learn something?" It measures how a new observation changes the estimated mutual information between what the system controls and what it observes. A sequential statistical test decides when that change is meaningful rather than random fluctuation.

MIS then drives a reaction policy. If knowledge is growing as expected, the system keeps going. If not, it can adjust its sampling or fork the process to explore a newly informative direction, without throwing away what it already knows.

The key question shifts from “Was that unlikely?” to “Did I learn something?”

What we found

In synthetic studies and a dynamic pollution-map estimation task, MIS-guided policies were more stable, more responsive to real change, and more accurate in prediction than policies based on classical surprise measures.
