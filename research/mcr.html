Research/A Theory of MCR

Learning with incomplete data

When does consistency help?

A theoretical account of why measure consistency regularization can improve learning with missing features or modalities—and why that benefit is not automatic.

Yinsong Wang · Shahin ShahrampourPreprint · 2026

Project 04

The problem

Modern datasets often contain missing features, corrupted observations, or absent modalities. A common strategy is to impute the missing information and add a regularizer that makes the distribution of completed samples resemble the distribution of fully observed samples. We call this family of methods Measure Consistency Regularization, or MCR.

MCR works well in applications including image inpainting, data imputation, and semi-supervised learning. Yet empirical success alone does not explain when the regularizer improves generalization—or when it can fail.

The idea

We analyze MCR through a neural network distance. The resulting bounds identify the term responsible for its generalization advantage under partial observability. Extending the analysis beyond idealized optimization shows a crucial caveat: imperfect training can erase the benefit.

The theory then suggests a practical training protocol. By monitoring the duality gap, training can stop at a point designed to preserve the regularizer’s generalization advantage.

A useful regularizer needs both the right statistical structure and the right stopping rule.

Why it matters

This analysis connects an intuitive modeling practice—make imputed data look like observed data—to a precise statement about generalization. It also turns theory into an observable training signal rather than leaving it as a post-hoc explanation.

Experiments across multiple architectures and real-world data simulations support the predicted behavior and show that the stopping criterion tracks the regime in which MCR remains beneficial.
