Incomplete · multimodal data

When does consistency help?

Making imputed data look like real data is a popular trick for learning with missing values. We explain why it works, and why the benefit is not automatic.

Yinsong Wang · Shahin ShahrampourPreprint · 2026

Project 03

Learning from data with holes

Engineering data are often incomplete: a sensor drops out, a test was skipped, or an entire modality is missing for some units. A popular recipe is to train a neural network to fill in the missing pieces, and to add a regularizer that makes the completed samples statistically indistinguishable from the fully observed ones. Think of a GAN-style critic that keeps checking, "does this filled-in sample look real?"

We call this family Measure Consistency Regularization (MCR). It powers methods for image inpainting, tabular data imputation, and semi-supervised learning, and it often works very well. But why it works, and when it does not, was not well understood.

What people usually do

In practice, MCR is treated as a helpful trick: add the regularizer, train until convergence, and hope that it improves generalization. When it helps, nobody is quite sure which ingredient helped. When it does not, there is no principled way to tell what went wrong.

Our idea: a theory of when consistency helps

We analyzed MCR through a neural network distance between distributions. This let us isolate the precise term responsible for MCR's generalization advantage under partial observability: fully observed samples act as an anchor that keeps the imputer honest.

Extending the analysis beyond idealized training revealed an important caveat. Imperfect optimization can erase that advantage. The theory then points to a practical fix: monitor the duality gap of the min-max training problem, and stop training at the point that preserves the regularizer's benefit.

A useful regularizer needs both the right statistical structure and the right stopping rule.

What we found

Experiments across multiple architectures and real-world data simulations supported the theory: the predicted behavior showed up in practice, and the duality-gap stopping criterion tracked the regime in which MCR stays beneficial.
